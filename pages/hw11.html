Wiener Process via Euler-Maruyama

1. The Wiener Process (Brownian Motion)

The Wiener Process, denoted as \( W_t \), is the fundamental building block of stochastic calculus. It is a continuous-time stochastic process characterized by three key properties:

  • Initialization: It starts at zero (\( W_0 = 0 \)).
  • Independent Increments: The future evolution of the process depends only on its current state, not on its past history (Markov property).
  • Gaussian Increments: The change in value over any time interval \( \Delta t \) follows a Normal distribution with mean 0 and variance \( \Delta t \): \[ W_{t+\Delta t} - W_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \Delta t) \]

2. Stochastic Differential Equations (SDE)

While standard calculus uses ordinary differential equations (ODEs) to model deterministic systems (like a falling apple), we use Stochastic Differential Equations (SDEs) to model systems affected by random noise (like a stock price).

The general form of an SDE is: \[ dX_t = \mu(X_t, t)dt + \sigma(X_t, t)dW_t \]

  • \( \mu(\cdot) dt \): The Drift term (the deterministic trend).
  • \( \sigma(\cdot) dW_t \): The Diffusion term (the random noise scaled by volatility).

3. The Euler-Maruyama Method

Most SDEs cannot be solved analytically. To simulate them on a computer, we must discretize time into \( N \) steps of size \( \Delta t = T/N \). The Euler-Maruyama method is the stochastic equivalent of the Euler method for ODEs.

For our specific case of a Generalized Wiener Process (where drift \(\mu\) and diffusion \(\sigma\) are constants), the SDE is: \[ dX_t = \mu dt + \sigma dW_t \]

The numerical approximation becomes the recursive formula used in our simulation: \[ X_{i+1} = X_i + \underbrace{\mu \Delta t}_{\text{Drift}} + \underbrace{\sigma \sqrt{\Delta t} \cdot Z_i}_{\text{Diffusion}} \]

Where \( Z_i \) is a standard normal random variable \( \mathcal{N}(0, 1) \), generated here using the Box-Muller transform. Note that \( \sqrt{\Delta t} \) is critical because the variance of Brownian motion scales linearly with time (\( t \)), so the standard deviation (volatility) scales with the square root of time (\( \sqrt{t} \)).
